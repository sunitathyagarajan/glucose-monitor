Inside a modern glucose sensor

A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) is an enzyme electrode on a flexible filament only a few hairs wide, worn under the skin for up to two weeks. It turns glucose molecules into electrons — and electrons into a number on your phone.

Insertion: a spring-loaded applicator pushes a hollow needle carrying the filament into the skin, then retracts the needle, leaving only the soft filament behind. Modern devices combine sensor, electronics and battery in one disposable unit.3,19

03

The electrode and its membranes

The sensing tip is an electrochemical cell. A potentiostat holds the working electrode at a fixed voltage and measures the tiny current that flows when glucose-derived molecules are oxidised.20

Three-electrode cell

Electrode roles

ElectrodeMaterialJob
Working (WE)Pt or Pt–Ir (oxygen-based); carbon/gold (mediated)Site of the sensing reaction; coated with enzyme and membranes
Reference (RE)Ag/AgClFixed, known potential; carries no current
Counter (CE)Pt, carbon or Ag/AgClCompletes the circuit; carries the current
Amperometry: the potential is fixed; the measured current i is proportional to the rate of the electrode reaction, which is proportional to glucose: i = S·[glucose] + i₀ (S = sensitivity, i₀ = background).

Many commercial CGMs use a two-electrode design in which Ag/AgCl serves as both reference and counter.1

04

The chemistry at the electrode

The recognition element is glucose oxidase (GOx), a flavoenzyme (from the mould Aspergillus niger) that is highly selective for β-D-glucose, robust and cheap.4,5 Its cofactor FAD shuttles two electrons per glucose.

Why the voltage matters

Oxidising H₂O₂ on Pt needs ≈ +0.6 V vs Ag/AgCl. At that potential paracetamol, ascorbate and urate are oxidised too — hence the interference-rejection layer. Mediated sensors work near 0 V and largely avoid this.1,2

The oxygen deficit

ISF contains roughly 50–100× more glucose than dissolved O₂. Without a barrier, O₂ runs out first and the signal stops rising. A glucose-limiting membrane lets O₂ through far more easily than glucose.21,2

Two electrons per glucose

Each glucose that reacts yields one H₂O₂ and therefore 2 e⁻. A current of 1 nA corresponds to ≈ 3 × 10⁹ glucose molecules per second (1 nA ÷ (2 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)).

05

From current to glucose

The enzyme follows saturating (Michaelis–Menten-like) kinetics and needs O₂. The outer membrane converts this curved response into a nearly straight line, so one sensitivity value can convert nA to mg/dL.2,9

Sensor calibrated at 100 mg/dL and normal O₂. Lower O₂ mimics pressure on the sensor (e.g. lying on it at night) → a false “compression low”. Model is illustrative.

Calibration

glucose = (i − i₀) / S

Older CGMs needed fingerstick readings twice a day to estimate S and i₀. Tight control of membrane thickness and enzyme loading in manufacturing now allows factory calibration — no fingersticks.9

Algorithms

On-board software filters noise, compensates for sensor drift and the ISF lag, flags faults, computes a trend arrow from the rate of change, and predicts lows before they happen.16

06

Blood vs interstitial fluid

Glucose must diffuse from capillaries into ISF, so ISF glucose trails blood glucose by about 5–10 minutes — most noticeable when glucose changes fast (after meals, exercise or insulin).10

dGISF/dt = (Gblood − GISF) / τ

Trend arrows (rate of change): → <1, ↗ 1–2, ↑ 2–3, ⇈ >3 mg/dL per min (and the mirror images when falling).

Time in range: what CGM data are used for

International consensus targets for most adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes:11

>70% in range

70–180 mg/dL (in range)

Each 5% more time in range is clinically meaningful. CGM data also feed automated insulin delivery (“hybrid closed-loop”) systems that adjust an insulin pump every few minutes.

Known limitations

  • Warm-up after insertion (30–60 min) while the tissue settles.
  • Foreign-body response: proteins and cells coat the sensor (biofouling), changing sensitivity over days.17
  • Interference from some drugs (e.g. high-dose vitamin C, hydroxyurea, paracetamol for some models) — check device labelling.
  • Lag during rapid changes; confirm symptoms with a fingerstick.

What’s next

Non-invasive

Near-IR, Raman or radio-frequency sensing through skin — still limited by low signal and skin variability.

Microneedles

Painless patches of sub-millimetre needles that sample ISF just below the surface.

Other fluids

Sweat, tears and saliva sensors; correlation with blood glucose remains the challenge.

Multi-analyte & closed loop

Glucose + ketone sensors and fully automated insulin delivery (“artificial pancreas”).

A–Z

Glossary

Ag/AgCl electrode
Silver coated with silver chloride; gives a stable, known potential and is used as the reference (and often counter) electrode.
Amperometry
Measuring the current that flows at a fixed electrode potential. The current is proportional to the rate of the electrode reaction.
Analyte
The substance being measured — here, glucose.
ASIC
Application-specific integrated circuit: the custom chip that acts as potentiostat, digitiser and processor inside the sensor.
Automated insulin delivery (hybrid closed loop)
A system in which CGM readings automatically adjust an insulin pump every few minutes.
Background current (i₀)
Current that flows even with no glucose; subtracted during calibration.
Biofouling / foreign-body response
Proteins and cells coating an implanted sensor, which can change its sensitivity over time.
Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE)
Low-power radio standard that sends readings to a phone, receiver or pump.
Boronic acid
An R–B(OH)₂ group that binds reversibly to the cis-diols of glucose; used in fluorescent (optical) sensors such as Eversense.
Calibration
Converting current to glucose using glucose = (i − i₀)/S. Done with fingersticks in older CGMs; set at the factory in most current ones.
CGM (continuous glucose monitor)
A wearable device that measures interstitial glucose every 1–5 minutes for days to weeks.
Compression low
A falsely low reading caused by pressure on the sensor (e.g. lying on it), which reduces local blood flow and O₂.
Counter electrode (CE)
Completes the circuit by carrying the current that flows through the working electrode.
Direct electron transfer (DET)
Electrons pass from the enzyme straight to the electrode with no O₂ or mediator (“3rd-generation” sensors).
FAD / FADH₂
Flavin adenine dinucleotide: the cofactor of glucose oxidase. It accepts two electrons and two protons from glucose (FAD → FADH₂).
Factory calibration
Sensor sensitivity set during manufacturing, so no fingerstick calibration is needed.
Fluorescence
Light emitted by a molecule after it absorbs light of a shorter wavelength; its intensity reports glucose in optical sensors.
Glucono-δ-lactone
The cyclic ester produced when glucose oxidase oxidises glucose; it hydrolyses to gluconic acid.
Glucose-limiting membrane
Outer polymer layer that slows glucose but lets O₂ through, keeping O₂ in excess so the signal is linear.
Glucose oxidase (GOx)
The enzyme in most glucose sensors: β-D-glucose + O₂ → gluconolactone + H₂O₂.
Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)
Product of the GOx reaction; oxidised at a platinum electrode (H₂O₂ → O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻) to give the signal.
iCGM
“Integrated CGM”: an FDA category for CGMs accurate enough to connect to insulin pumps and other devices.
Interference-rejection layer
Thin film next to the electrode that lets H₂O₂ through but blocks larger electroactive molecules.
Interferent
A substance other than glucose that is oxidised at the electrode and inflates the signal, e.g. paracetamol, ascorbate, urate.
Interstitial fluid (ISF)
The fluid surrounding cells under the skin, where CGMs measure glucose.
Lag time
The delay (≈ 5–10 min) between a change in blood glucose and the same change in interstitial glucose.
MARD
Mean absolute relative difference between CGM and reference readings; lower means more accurate.
Mediator
A redox molecule (ferrocene, ferricyanide, osmium complex) that carries electrons from the enzyme to the electrode in place of O₂.
Michaelis–Menten kinetics
Enzyme rate rises with substrate concentration and then levels off (saturates).
Nanoamp (nA)
10⁻⁹ ampere: the typical size of a CGM signal current.
Non-enzymatic sensor
Oxidises glucose directly on a metal or metal-oxide surface without an enzyme; mostly at the research stage.
Oxygen deficit
Tissue has far less O₂ than glucose, so a bare GOx electrode runs out of O₂ and its signal stops rising.
Potentiostat
Circuit that holds the working electrode at a set potential relative to the reference electrode and measures the current.
Reference electrode (RE)
Electrode with a stable, known potential against which the working electrode is controlled; it carries no current.
Sensitivity (S)
Signal per unit glucose, e.g. nA per mg/dL; the slope of the calibration line.
Time in range (TIR)
Percentage of time glucose is 70–180 mg/dL; the consensus target is above 70%.
Trend arrow
Arrow showing how fast and in which direction glucose is changing (mg/dL per minute).
Warm-up period
Time after insertion (≈ 30–60 min) before readings are reported, while the sensor and tissue stabilise.
Wired enzyme
Enzyme linked to the electrode through a redox polymer (e.g. osmium-based) that conducts electrons; used in FreeStyle Libre.
Working electrode (WE)
The electrode where the sensing reaction occurs; coated with enzyme and membranes.
