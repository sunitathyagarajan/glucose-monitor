01

Sugars & saccharides

Carbohydrates (“saccharides”, from Greek sákcharon, sugar) are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, with a general formula close to Cn(H2O)n. They are classified by how many sugar units they contain.1

Monosaccharides

1 unit · cannot be hydrolysed further
glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose

Disaccharides

2 units joined by a glycosidic bond
maltose, lactose, sucrose

Oligosaccharides

3–10 units
raffinose (beans)

Polysaccharides

>10 units (often thousands)
starch, glycogen, cellulose

Classifying monosaccharides

Two questions help determine the name of a monosaccharide: where is the C=O? and how many carbons?

So glucose is an aldohexose and fructose a ketohexose — same formula (C6H12O6), different carbonyl position.

Reducing vs. non-reducing sugars

Rule: a sugar is reducing if it has a free anomeric carbon (a hemiacetal). The ring can open to an aldehyde that donates electrons to Cu²⁺ or Ag⁺.1,2

If every anomeric carbon is tied up in a glycosidic bond (an acetal), the ring is locked → non-reducing.

Glucose up close

Glucose is the main sugar in blood and the primary fuel for the brain. Its structure explains every test and sensor in this module.2

Aldose vs. ketose: Glucose and fructose

Fischer projections. Horizontal bonds point toward you; each cross is a carbon. The –OH on the last chiral carbon (C5) on the right makes both sugars D-sugars.

D-Glucose
aldose: C1 = –CHO
D-Fructose
ketose: C2 = C=O

C3–C6 are identical; only C1–C2 differ.

Aldose ⇌ ketose: How glucose becomes fructose

Ring formation, anomers & mutarotation

In water, the C5–OH attacks the C1 aldehyde to form a six-membered pyranose ring (a hemiacetal). C1 becomes a new chiral centre — the anomeric carbon — giving two anomers.1

α-D-glucopyranose
C1–OH down (trans to CH₂OH)
β-D-glucopyranose
C1–OH up (cis to CH₂OH)
α-D-glucose ⇌ open-chain aldehyde ⇌ β-D-glucose

β dominates because, in the chair, all its –OH groups are equatorial (check the 3D model).

At equilibrium ≈ 36% α, 64% β, <1% open chain. Only the open chain reacts in reducing-sugar tests, but it is constantly regenerated.1 (Rate shown is illustrative.)

Traditional tests for glucose

Classical tests use three ideas: reduction of metal ions by the free aldehyde (Cu²⁺ → Cu⁺, Ag⁺ → Ag), dehydration of sugars by strong acid into coloured furfural products, and enzymes that react with glucose alone.

Advances in glucose testing

Over ~2,500 years glucose testing moved from tasting urine, to copper chemistry in test tubes, to enzyme strips, to electrodes that read glucose every few minutes. Click a milestone.

Generations of electrochemical glucose sensors

1st gen

Oxygen + H₂O₂

Glucose oxidase uses O₂; the electrode detects the H₂O₂ made. Clark (1962), Updike & Hicks (1967).16,17

2nd gen

Mediators

An artificial electron shuttle (ferrocene, ferricyanide, osmium complexes) replaces O₂. Basis of most test strips.18

3rd gen

Direct electron transfer

The enzyme passes electrons straight to a (nano-structured) electrode — no mediator.21

“4th gen”

Non-enzymatic

Glucose is oxidised directly on metal / metal-oxide nanostructures (Pt, Au, Ni, Cu). Mostly research.22

Glossary

Acetal
A carbon bonded to two –OR groups. Formed when a hemiacetal reacts with another alcohol; glycosidic bonds are acetals and cannot open.
Aldose
A monosaccharide whose carbonyl is an aldehyde (–CHO) at C1. Example: glucose.
Amylase
Enzyme in saliva and pancreatic juice that breaks starch into maltose and short chains.
Amylose / amylopectin
The two parts of starch: amylose is unbranched α1→4 glucose; amylopectin also has α1→6 branches.
Anomeric carbon
The carbon that was the carbonyl carbon before ring closure (C1 in glucose, C2 in fructose). It carries two oxygens.
Anomers
Ring forms that differ only at the anomeric carbon, e.g. α- and β-D-glucose.
Barfoed’s test
Copper(II) acetate in dilute acid; gives a red precipitate within ~2 min with monosaccharides but not disaccharides.
Benedict’s test
Alkaline copper(II)–citrate reagent; reducing sugars give green → brick-red Cu₂O. Semi-quantitative.
Biosensor
A device that couples a biological recognition element (e.g. an enzyme) to a transducer that produces a measurable signal.
Chair conformation
The puckered, strain-free 3D shape of a six-membered ring. In β-D-glucose every –OH is equatorial.
Chiral centre
A carbon bonded to four different groups, so its mirror images cannot be superimposed.
Copper(I) oxide (Cu₂O)
The insoluble brick-red precipitate formed when Cu²⁺ is reduced in Benedict’s, Fehling’s and Barfoed’s tests.
CGM (continuous glucose monitor)
A wearable sensor that measures glucose in interstitial fluid every 1–5 minutes. See the sensor page.
D / L configuration
Based on the –OH of the highest-numbered chiral carbon in a Fischer projection: on the right = D, on the left = L. Natural glucose is D.
Disaccharide
Two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond, e.g. maltose, lactose, sucrose.
Enediol
An intermediate with a C=C double bond carrying an –OH on each carbon. It links glucose, fructose and mannose in base.
Enzyme electrode
An electrode coated with an enzyme so that the enzyme’s reaction produces an electrical signal. The basis of glucose meters and CGMs.
Epimers
Sugars that differ at only one chiral carbon, e.g. glucose and galactose (C4), glucose and mannose (C2).
Fehling’s test
Strongly alkaline copper(II)–tartrate reagent; reducing sugars give a brick-red Cu₂O precipitate.
Fischer projection
A 2D drawing of an open-chain sugar: vertical carbon chain, horizontal bonds pointing toward the viewer.
Furanose
A five-membered sugar ring (four C + one O), e.g. fructose in sucrose.
Furfural / HMF
Furan aldehydes formed when strong acid dehydrates pentoses (furfural) or hexoses (5-hydroxymethylfurfural); they make the coloured products in Molisch’s and Seliwanoff’s tests.
Gluconic acid
The carboxylic acid formed when the C1 aldehyde of glucose is oxidised.
Glucose oxidase (GOx / GOD)
A flavoenzyme that oxidises β-D-glucose using O₂, producing gluconolactone and H₂O₂. Highly specific for glucose.
Glycogen
Highly branched glucose polymer; the animal storage form of glucose (liver, muscle).
Glycosidic bond
The C–O–C bond that links the anomeric carbon of one sugar to an –OH of another.
GOD-POD (Trinder) test
Glucose oxidase makes H₂O₂; peroxidase uses it to form a red quinoneimine dye. Specific for glucose.
Haworth projection
A flat drawing of a sugar ring seen edge-on, with groups shown above or below the ring.
Hemiacetal / hemiketal
A carbon bonded to one –OH and one –OR, formed when an alcohol adds to an aldehyde (hemiacetal) or ketone (hemiketal). It can reopen.
Iodine test
Iodine/iodide turns blue-black with starch because polyiodide fits inside the amylose helix.
Ketose
A monosaccharide whose carbonyl is a ketone, usually at C2. Example: fructose.
Lobry de Bruyn–van Ekenstein transformation
Base-catalysed interconversion of aldoses and ketoses through an enediol.
Maltase
Intestinal enzyme that hydrolyses maltose into two glucose molecules.
Mediator
A small redox molecule (e.g. ferrocene, ferricyanide) that shuttles electrons from an enzyme to an electrode.
mg/dL and mmol/L
Units of blood glucose. 1 mmol/L = 18 mg/dL (molar mass of glucose ≈ 180 g/mol).
Molisch’s test
General test for carbohydrates: α-naphthol + concentrated H₂SO₄ gives a purple ring.
Monosaccharide
A single sugar unit that cannot be hydrolysed further, e.g. glucose.
Mutarotation
The change in optical rotation as α and β anomers interconvert (via the open chain) until equilibrium.
Non-reducing sugar
A sugar with no free anomeric carbon, so its ring cannot open to an aldehyde. Example: sucrose.
Oligosaccharide
A short chain of about 3–10 sugar units, e.g. raffinose.
Osazone
Yellow crystalline derivative formed with phenylhydrazine; crystal shape helps identify sugars.
Peroxidase (POD)
An enzyme that uses H₂O₂ to oxidise a dye precursor, producing a coloured product.
Polysaccharide
A long chain of many sugar units, e.g. starch, glycogen, cellulose.
Pyranose
A six-membered sugar ring (five C + one O), e.g. glucopyranose.
Reducing sugar
A sugar with a free anomeric carbon whose ring can open and reduce Cu²⁺ or Ag⁺. Examples: glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose.
Saccharide
Another name for a carbohydrate or sugar (Greek sákcharon, sugar).
Seliwanoff’s test
Resorcinol in HCl; ketoses give a cherry-red colour within about a minute, aldoses much more slowly.
Specific rotation [α]D
The angle a sugar solution rotates plane-polarised light, standardised for concentration and path length.
Tollens’ test
Diammine-silver(I) reagent; aldehydes and reducing sugars deposit a silver mirror.
